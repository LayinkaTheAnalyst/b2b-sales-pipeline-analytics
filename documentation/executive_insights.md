# Executive Insights Report

## The short version

MavenTech’s completed sales performance looks healthy. The business won **4,238 of 6,711 completed opportunities**, giving it a **63.2% win rate**, and generated just over **$10 million in revenue** from won deals.

The strongest part of the story is the premium product portfolio. `GTX Pro` and `GTX Plus Pro` generated more than **$6.1 million combined**, or roughly **61% of total revenue**. Selling prices also stayed very close to the catalogue prices, suggesting that MavenTech is not relying on heavy discounting to close business.

The biggest concern is the age of the open pipeline. Of the 1,589 opportunities currently in the Engaging stage, **1,291—or 81.2%—have been open for more than 138 days**. That matters because 138 days is already the longest sales cycle seen among completed opportunities. These deals are not necessarily lost, but they deserve a deliberate review.

## How the business is performing overall

MavenTech recorded **8,800 opportunities** in total:

| Outcome | Opportunities |
|---|---:|
| Won | 4,238 |
| Lost | 2,473 |
| Engaging | 1,589 |
| Prospecting | 500 |
| **Total** | **8,800** |

I calculated win rate using completed opportunities only—Won divided by Won plus Lost. Open opportunities were left out because they do not yet have a final outcome.

On that basis, MavenTech’s **63.2% win rate** means it wins nearly two out of every three opportunities that reach a decision. The average won deal is worth **$2,360.91**, while completed opportunities take an average of **48 days** to close.

There is also an interesting monthly rhythm. March, June, September and December produced noticeably higher win rates and revenue than the months immediately following them. Those stronger months line up with quarter ends, which may suggest a quarter-end closing push or a reporting-cycle effect. I would not treat that as a confirmed explanation without speaking to the sales team, but it is worth highlighting in the dashboard.

## Where the revenue comes from

The product results show why opportunity counts alone can be misleading.

| Product | Won deals | Win rate | Won revenue |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| GTX Pro | 729 | 63.6% | $3,510,578 |
| GTX Plus Pro | 479 | 64.3% | $2,629,651 |
| MG Advanced | 654 | 60.3% | $2,216,387 |
| GTX Plus Basic | 653 | 62.1% | $705,275 |
| GTX Basic | 915 | 63.7% | $499,263 |
| GTK 500 | 15 | 60.0% | $400,612 |
| MG Special | 793 | 64.8% | $43,768 |

`MG Special` won the most business by conversion rate and had 793 won deals, yet it contributed less than $44,000 because its average selling price was only about $55. At the other extreme, `GTK 500` generated more than $400,000 from just 15 wins because each sale was worth roughly $26,700.

This is why product performance should be viewed through at least three lenses: conversion, revenue and deal value. `GTX Pro` stands out as the strongest all-round revenue contributor, while `GTX Plus Pro` and `MG Advanced` are also commercially important.

## Pricing appears disciplined

Average selling prices were within roughly 1.5% of suggested prices for every product. Some products sold at a small average discount, while others closed at a slight premium.

That suggests MavenTech is generally holding its price rather than buying revenue through aggressive discounting. I would keep price realization as a visible KPI in the dashboard, but the current data does not point to a broad pricing problem.

The variation within each product may still reveal useful negotiation patterns. A future drill-down could show which agents, customers or regions discount most often, even when the company-wide average looks stable.

## The regional picture is balanced—but each office wins differently

| Regional office | Opportunities | Win rate | Won revenue | Average won deal |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| West | 2,997 | 63.9% | $3,568,647 | $2,481.67 |
| Central | 3,512 | 62.6% | $3,346,293 | $2,054.20 |
| East | 2,291 | 63.0% | $3,090,594 | $2,639.28 |

West generated the most revenue and had the highest regional win rate. Central handled the largest opportunity volume, but its average won deal was the smallest of the three regions. East handled the fewest opportunities but produced the largest average deal value.

This suggests different management questions for each office:

- **Central:** Can its large activity base be shifted toward higher-value opportunities?
- **East:** Can its strong deal values be supported with more opportunity volume?
- **West:** Which practices are helping it combine healthy conversion with the highest revenue?

The regional differences are meaningful, but none of the offices appears fundamentally weak. The better opportunity is to understand and share what each region does well.

## Agent performance needs context, not just a leaderboard

Darcel Schlecht generated the most individual revenue at approximately **$1.15 million**. That result came from a combination of high opportunity volume and an average won deal of more than $3,300—not from having the highest win rate.

Among agents with at least 30 completed opportunities, Hayden Neloms had the highest win rate at **70.4%**, closely followed by Maureen Marcano at **70.0%**. These agents may offer useful coaching examples around qualification, follow-up or closing discipline.

This is an important distinction. An agent can lead revenue because they manage more opportunities, win more consistently or close larger deals. The dashboard should keep those drivers separate so leadership does not reward volume as if it were conversion—or penalize an agent who works a smaller but more valuable portfolio.

Five rostered agents had no recorded opportunities. I have retained them in the model because the data does not tell us whether they were new, inactive or outside the reporting period. They should be shown as having no recorded activity, not treated as poor performers.

## Customer revenue is diversified

Retail generated the most revenue at approximately **$1.87 million**, followed by technology at **$1.52 million** and medical at **$1.36 million**. Much of this advantage comes from opportunity volume rather than a dramatically higher win rate; sector win rates mostly sit within a fairly narrow range.

The largest customer account, Kan-code, contributed about **$341,000**. The top five accounts together produced only **12.1% of company revenue**, and the top ten produced **20.7%**.

That is a reassuring concentration result. MavenTech does not appear overly dependent on a small number of customers, which reduces the impact of losing any single account.

Larger organizations naturally contributed most of the revenue because they generated most of the opportunities. Small companies had the highest win rate at **67.3%**, but their lower volume and smaller average deal size limited their total revenue contribution. That segment may still offer efficient wins if the cost of serving it is low, although this dataset does not include cost or profit information.

## The open pipeline is the main management concern

MavenTech has **2,089 open opportunities**: 500 in Prospecting and 1,589 in Engaging.

I could only age the Engaging opportunities because Prospecting records do not have a creation or engagement date. Using December 31, 2017—the latest date in the dataset—as the reporting snapshot, the average Engaging opportunity had been open for **198.8 days**.

More importantly, **1,291 Engaging opportunities had remained open for over 138 days**. Since no completed opportunity took longer than 138 days, I classified these as potentially stalled.

This does not prove that the opportunities are dead. It tells us they are behaving differently from every successfully completed or lost deal in the available history. Leadership should review whether they are still active, need support or should be closed out.

The issue is spread across the organization, although the proportions vary:

- Melvin Marxen’s team: 213 potentially stalled opportunities
- Dustin Brinkmann’s team: 190
- Summer Sewald’s team: 305
- Celia Rouche’s team: 257
- Cara Losch’s team: 169
- Rocco Neubert’s team: 157

Central’s two teams have especially high stalled shares—over 98% of their Engaging opportunities—while the other teams range from roughly 73% to 77%. This difference deserves investigation, but it could reflect process or timestamp practices rather than salesperson effort alone.

There is also an account-assignment issue. **1,425 open opportunities do not yet have a named account**, including 1,088 in Engaging. An unnamed account may be reasonable early in Prospecting, but it becomes more concerning after engagement has started because it limits customer-level tracking and follow-up.

## What I would recommend

### 1. Run a focused pipeline clean-up

Review the 1,291 Engaging opportunities older than 138 days. Confirm whether each deal is still active, needs a next action or should be closed. This would give leadership a more realistic picture of current pipeline health.

### 2. Improve account assignment

Prioritize the 1,088 Engaging opportunities without a named account. Requiring an account once an opportunity moves into Engaging would improve ownership, customer history and account-level reporting.

### 3. Protect the premium-product engine

`GTX Pro`, `GTX Plus Pro` and `MG Advanced` are the core revenue drivers. Sales leadership should monitor their opportunity flow, conversion and sales-cycle length closely. Any deterioration in these products would have an outsized effect on total revenue.

### 4. Use coaching based on performance drivers

Avoid relying on a single agent leaderboard. Use revenue, conversion, average deal value and pipeline health together. High-conversion agents may offer closing lessons, while high-revenue agents may demonstrate effective management of large or premium opportunities.

### 5. Investigate the quarter-end pattern

The recurring spikes in March, June, September and December could reflect genuine customer buying behaviour, internal sales targets or data-entry timing. Understanding the cause would improve forecasting and help leadership decide whether the pattern is healthy or creates avoidable end-of-quarter pressure.

## What this means for the dashboard

The findings support the three-page Power BI structure defined in the business brief:

1. **Executive Overview** — revenue, win rate, average deal value, closed and open opportunities, monthly trends and regional performance.
2. **Sales Team and Pipeline Performance** — agent and manager scorecards, revenue drivers, open pipeline, ageing bands and stalled-opportunity indicators.
3. **Product and Account Insights** — product revenue and conversion, price realization, sectors, company size, top accounts and revenue concentration.

The dashboard should make the healthy parts of the business easy to see, but it should also keep the ageing pipeline visible. That is the clearest area where the analysis points toward immediate management action.

## A note on interpretation

This analysis is based on a fictional CRM dataset and should be treated as a decision-support exercise rather than a complete commercial diagnosis. The data does not include profit, quotas, acquisition costs, estimated values for open opportunities or probability-weighted forecasts.

Even with those limitations, the available evidence tells a useful story: MavenTech converts completed opportunities well, protects its pricing and has a diversified customer base—but it needs a clearer, more disciplined view of which open opportunities are genuinely still alive.
